1. What Changed Since the First Plan
The first business plan was written when this whole thing was an idea on paper. It promised a machine
that would build websites by itself, find its own customers, and cost nothing to run. It answered questions
like what to build, who would do which part, and whether the math could possibly work. That was its job,
and it did that job. This plan has a different job, because the situation has changed underneath it.
The machine now exists. The whole factory arrived as a folder of sixty-one files: the website design, the
scripts that turn a spreadsheet row into a finished site, five sets of automation instructions, installers for
both a Mac and a Windows laptop, the setup walkthrough, and a finished example site that was built by
the real tools, not by hand. Every item the first plan listed under "the assistant builds it" has been built,
checked, and delivered. Nothing on that side of the ledger is still open.
Two things changed that reshaped this plan. First, the original assumed the operator would wait for a new
MacBook before anything started; instead, the factory was made to run on the Windows laptop that is
here today, so the clock can start immediately. Second, the risk profile flipped. "Can it be built for free"
is now an answered question. The only question left is the ordinary one every business faces: will people
buy. Everything in the pages that follow is aimed at that question.

2. The Business in One Look
For anyone reading this fresh at the kitchen table, here is the whole idea in one breath. Charm City Sites
builds finished websites for Baltimore's home-service trades before any money changes hands, shows the
finished thing to the owner, and sells it the same day for a flat one-time price. Alongside it sits a second
small product: a missed-call helper that texts back within seconds when the owner cannot answer the
phone, so a ringing job never quietly dies. The first trade is plumbers, because the city's aging rowhomes
guarantee a steady drip of burst pipes and flooded basements.
The buyer is a plumber who is excellent at pipes and short on spare hours, whose customers check a
screen before dialing, and who would rather look at a finished thing than imagine a proposal. The pitch is
honest and short: "I built you a website, no strings. It is yours for five hundred dollars if you want it, live
today." A neighbor handing over something real, with the plumber's own name already on it, gets replies
that no remote stranger pitching "online presence" ever gets.

4. The Factory, As Built
Here is what physically exists today: a single folder that turns a laptop into the factory. Unzip it,
double-click one installer, and the machine assembles itself - the tools come in, the five workflows load,
and everything wakes up with the computer from then on. No code gets written by hand at any point, on
either a Mac or the Windows laptop.
The product side is a finished website design that accepts new businesses the way a cookie cutter accepts
new dough. Every site gets a big tap-to-call button, a plain services list, a quote form that alerts the
owner's phone within a minute, a reviews section, the details search engines read, and one of four rotating
color schemes so no two plumbers look identical. The writing is done by a free artificial-intelligence
service: the builder reads each spreadsheet row, asks for every word the site needs, and falls back first to
a second free service and then to a safe pre-written default, so the line never stalls. Anything the machine
is unsure about gets flagged for a human read instead of guessed at.
Around the product sit the five automation workflows that run the business itself. One watches the
spreadsheet and turns every new row into a built, live website. One hunts for new plumbers across the
map listings and the public licensing rolls, then enriches each lead with a phone number and email
address. One writes and sends the daily outreach, with gentle follow-ups on day three and day seven, and
stops politely when someone says no. One invoices closed deals on its own and offers the $400
missed-call helper two days after any sale. The last one watches the contact forms and the reply inbox,
and pushes hot leads to the operator's phone within a minute. A finished example site, built end-to-end by
the real tools and inspected on both a desktop and a phone screen, is sitting in the folder ready to show a
prospect today.

5. What Is Left: The 45-Minute Ignition
The factory is built but not yet switched on. Switching it on is the only real work left, and it is not
technical work; it is account-connecting work, done once and never again. The total is about forty-five
minutes of clicking, spread across four free accounts, following a page-by-page walkthrough that was
written for exactly this moment.
The steps are short. Five minutes to create the spreadsheet from two template files. Seven minutes to
open a fresh outreach Gmail and generate its app password. Twenty minutes - the biggest chunk - to
connect the control panel to Google, Telegram, and the mailbox. Five minutes for a free Cloudflare
account and its token, which is what puts finished sites on the internet. Eight minutes to paste the AI
brain's key and fill in one settings tab. Two of these are already on the operator's personal to-do list from
the first plan - the Cloudflare account and the email credentials - and they simply live inside these
forty-five minutes now.

8. Windows Now, MacBook Later
The first plan assumed the factory would wait for a new MacBook. The reality is better: the Windows
laptop on the desk today runs the entire machine, unchanged, because Windows carries a full Linux layer
inside it and the factory lives in that layer. Double-clicking one installer file, and approving one
administrator prompt, turns the whole thing on. If Windows asks to restart once to switch that layer on,
that is a once-ever event, and no Linux password is ever typed - the installer quietly makes its own hidden
working user.
It also heals itself. Two invisible maintenance tasks were created alongside the install: one checks every
five minutes that the factory's parts are alive and restarts anything that crashed, and it survives reboots,
waking the machine within five minutes of login; the other runs the daily 7:40 lead hunt as a
belt-and-suspenders backup. Closing the laptop overnight is fine - the heartbeat catches everything up
when it opens again. A double-click dashboard file answers "what is up, what is down, and what to paste
where" any time something looks wrong.

9. Risks: The Honest Ones That Remain
The first plan carried a big honest risk: this machine had never been built, and free tools sometimes
disappoint. That risk is retired - the factory exists, it was tested end to end, and it costs nothing to run.
What remains are the ordinary risks of selling things to strangers, and each one comes with a patch
already attached.
• The first yes may be slow. If no plumber has said yes by around day fourteen, the machine's own
records show which messages got opened and which got ignored, so the wording or the friend price
can be adjusted with evidence instead of guesswork. The $500 entry price exists precisely to make
the first yes easy.
• Rejection volume is the tax, not the failure. Most people will say no or say nothing, and the plan is
priced for that: a hundred touches containing three yeses is the machine working exactly as designed.
Quiet weeks in the middle are expected; the calendar, not the mood, is the antidote.
• Machine-written words can occasionally be off-key. Every site the builder is unsure about gets
flagged for a human read, and a pre-written default sits underneath as a quality floor, so the worst
case is a plain site, never a broken one.
• Free tools can change their terms. The factory uses interchangeable parts behind one standard
connection, so a swapped provider is an evening of adjustment, not a rebuild, and every account stays
in the operator's own name.
• The laptop is the factory floor. The self-healing keeps it running, a weekly backup habit means a
stolen machine costs a day plus the fifteen-minute migration, and the phone keeps commanding
everything in between.
• Enthusiasm fades on hard weeks. The machine removes the excuse: the minimum viable day is
answering what already rang, because the ten touches went out whether or not anyone felt inspired.
The one failure with no patch is the same one the first plan named: sending nothing. Every other item on
this list has a response bolted to it. The factory's deepest purpose is to make silence impossible on the
outreach side, so that the only human job left is the one humans are actually good at - picking up the
phone when it rings.

10. After the First $2,350
The forty-five-day clock ends with money in the account and three live storefronts on the internet
carrying the Charm City Sites signature. The second act is repetition with variation: point the same
factory at electricians, then heating-and-cooling shops, then roofers, one trade at a time. Nothing gets
rebuilt; the machine simply gets new rows, and every lesson the spreadsheet records about which words,
prices, and neighborhoods convert makes the next yes cheaper to earn than the last.
Two quiet upgrades follow naturally, exactly as the first plan sketched them. A small monthly care plan,
offered only to clients who ask, covers tiny edits and keeps phone numbers current, and even a handful of
those turns lumpy income steadier. The missed-call helper grows into a fuller after-hours assistant that
answers common questions and books jobs while the owner sleeps, which tradespeople pay for gladly
because it works the hours they cannot.
And the moat compounds on its own. The machine stays private, the lead sheet grows into a private
record of what actually works in this city, the testimonials stack up faster than any copycat can fake them,
and the name keeps being the same name. Growth, if it comes, arrives because demand pulls it, not
because the operator pushes.
